# 0030 — A brand's length column read by its grid: a label headed as the foot gives way to ISO's label where the labs corroborate

Date: 2026-10-21
Status: accepted

## Context

The brands' charts (ADR 0028) fill the product's brand rows as printed, each row disclosing the tier that filled it (docs/RESEARCH.md §9): the brand's own foot length, its own stated rule, or ISO's label where it prints no length. The sources pass (docs/research/shoe-sizes-3.md) read two labs that measure shoes inside, RunRepeat's caliper on the cut shoe and RTINGS' scanned footbed, 167 and 72 shoes at a US men's 9, and found every brand's shoe within a third of a size of the pack while the brands' printed feet for that size spread a size and a half, in two families. US and UK sizes are graded at a third of an inch, 8.47 mm, per full size, so a column stepping a round centimetre a size at round numbers (27 at a US 9) cannot be a measured foot across the range: it is the standard centimetre label every conversion table prints, which Nike prints beside its foot column 0.8 cm apart and which On and Asics describe as the foot plus about a centimetre. New Balance, Under Armour, Vans, Crocs, Timberland, Dr. Martens, and UGG head that label as the foot; a visitor following one of those charts at a 27 cm foot buys a shoe a full size tighter than the same foot gets at Nike, and a visitor converting a Nike 10 to those brands through the charts gets a 9 for a shoe the labs find a size shorter inside. Sean's principle (the findings log, the hundred-and-thirty-third pass, 2026-10-21): a brand's print is the frame of reference until strong evidence contradicts it; then the product represents what the evidence says, says so, and cites its path.

## Decision

1. **A length column is classified by its grid before its words.** A column stepping ISO's barleycorn, with unround values, is a foot's (Nike, adidas, HOKA, Salomon, Clarks, Reebok's US page, Brooks' UK page). A column stepping a round centimetre a size at round numbers is the standard centimetre label, whatever its heading. The test is written into docs/RESEARCH.md §9 and the record's `brands.py` (`basis` `label`), and every chart of a later pass is classified so before it is read.
2. **A label headed as the foot gives way to ISO's label where three or more measured shoes of the brand corroborate the pattern.** New Balance, Under Armour, and Vans read by ISO's label (tier 4), the row saying `by ISO’s label`, the note saying why in one sentence, the About page citing the page, the pattern, and the labs. Puma and Skechers, which print no foot, stay there. A brand measured by no lab, or thinly (Crocs, Timberland, Dr. Martens, UGG, Converse), stays as printed, the pattern noted in the record; the wearers' flags say the boots and clogs run large, which their columns as printed already give, so an override there would rest on nothing measured.
3. **A brand's own rule outranks an inference from its steps.** Asics's NZ and AU pages say to add 1 to 1.5 cm to the foot to read its CM column; its US page's steps only compare the foot with the chart. The column is its label and the foot the label less a centimetre (tier 2, as On's Japan column), the lower end of its range and the figure Nike's and On's labels show, the note quoting both pages. The record carries the label in the row's `label` field, which is never listed as a scale, since the page heads it CM and not JP.
4. **A brand's other page fills what its home page lacks, joined on the size the pages share and checked row by row.** Brooks' UK page's Foot Length (CM) joins its home charts at the US size, its UK and EU cells equal on every row, so Brooks reads at tier 1. Reebok's AU men's and women's charts give its home guides their UK and EU, each line its own, since the two AU charts disagree on EU at the women's 5 to 7.5; the AU page's lengths, another grid, are never read.
5. **The labs' figures fill no row.** They corroborate, flag, and calibrate (docs/RESEARCH.md §9, tier 5). The one product line they earn is a fact under the table: measured inside, a US 9 is about the same length at every brand; the charts differ by the room each brand leaves.

## Alternatives

- **Every chart as printed, the pattern in a note alone.** Honest to the print, but it leaves the visitor the mental step the product exists to remove, a full size at the label-grid brands, and contradicts the labs where they measured. Lost on the product's value.
- **Every label-grid chart overridden, the unmeasured brands included.** Consistent, but it would rest on the pattern alone for boots and clogs whose lasts no lab measured and whose wearers report the opposite direction. Lost on the measured rule's threshold.
- **A measured offset per brand, Asics a quarter size long, Converse a half.** The offsets are within the half size the rows show, except Converse's, thin at two shoes and already given by its chart and its own advice. Lost on precision the data does not have.
- **Asics by the US page's reading, the column the foot.** Equidistant from the two families by one lab and nearer the roomy family by the other, but it puts a 27 cm foot in an Asics 9 three millimetres longer than the foot by the caliper, a full size apart from Nike's and ISO's 10 for the same foot, and it reads an inference over the brand's own sentence. Lost on the rule's hierarchy.

## Consequences

Easier: a visitor's foot or fitting size gives the same label across the athletic brands, as the labs say it should, and the charts' two families no longer put the same foot a size apart between Nike and New Balance. The record classifies a new chart in one step, so the second batch and the kids' charts start from the grid test. Harder: three brands' rows now differ from their own printed guides by a size, disclosed in the row and explained in the note; a visitor who checks will find the brand saying 9 where we say 10, and the About page must carry the path. The tiers' words in the rows stay for now and are Sean's to revise at the strings pass.
