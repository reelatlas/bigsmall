# Weight of one US cup of 34 baking ingredients: reviewer B

Reviewer B, working blind to bigsmall's figures and to reviewer A. Pages read 2026-10-16. 1 US cup = 236.5882365 mL; 1 oz = 28.349523125 g. 217 figures; every one is in `figures.csv` with its arithmetic, quote, URL and notes. Grams per cup below are per US customary cup.

## Sources

**USDA ARS, _Average Weight of a Measured Cup of Various Foods_, Home Economics Research Report No. 41** (Fulton, Matthews, Davis; first issued as ARS 61-6, Feb 1969; reissued Mar 1977; archive.org scan). USDA's Consumer and Food Economics Institute weighed 824 food items in the lab: dry foods "spooned lightly into the cup" filled to the rim and "leveled with the thin edge of a metal spatula" unless dipping or packing is named; liquids in glass cups "filled so that the meniscus was on the 1-cup mark"; weighed to the nearest gram; mean, SD and n (2 to 510) per item. The flour rows come from Matthews & Batcher, _J. Home Econ._ 55:123 (1963). This _is_ USDA's own measurement basis: the report says CFEI data "were also used to derive the nutritive value of foods in common household measures" (HG-72). Quirk: its liquids, especially viscous ones, weigh 1-7% less than their densities allow.

**King Arthur Baking, Ingredient Weight Chart** (and its flour-measuring guide and blog, 2023). House standards used in KA recipes; flour by "fluff, sprinkle, and scrape" = 120 g; dry ingredients spooned gently and swept; water, milk, cream, sour cream and yogurt written as 8 oz per cup (a fluid-ounce convention, 227 g); oils and syrups get their own rounded figures. Ounces in 1/8-oz steps with grams derived. Independent of USDA.

**America's Test Kitchen / Cook's Illustrated** (conversion chart, support article, recipe pages). Dip-and-sweep: "we had dozens of volunteers measure out 1 cup, weighed the results, and took the average (5 ounces) as our standard." Chart covers flours, sugars, cocoa and butter; other weights read off recipe ingredient lists (oats, chips, nuts, peanut butter, cheese, oil, bread flour). Milk and water written by fluid ounce. Independent of USDA.

**FAO/INFOODS Density Database v2.0 (2012)** (Charrondiere, Haytowitz, Stadlmayr; xlsx and user guide). A compilation of 638 densities from 11 sources: ASI (an industrial bulk-handling chart), RC (the first author's own measurements, 21 C, France), KEN (a Kenyan field project), DK (Danish tables), UK 6th (McCance & Widdowson), TB (hypertextbook.com), CSG (a web specific-gravity chart), USDA (measured specific gravities), and S&W and FNDDS, both back-calculated from USDA data and so **not independent**. No honey or molasses; baking soda is given as a crystal density.

**USDA Food Buying Guide for Child Nutrition Programs** (FNS, Sections 1, 4, 6). Purchase-unit yields such as "1 lb dry = about 6 cups"; coarse (1/8 to 1/3 cup steps) and of USDA lineage (it matches HERR 41 wherever both exist); a few entries are crediting rules, not measurements (yogurt "1 cup or 8 oz").

**Physical references.** NIST Chemistry WebBook (IAPWS-95) for water; Codex CXS 210-1999 (named vegetable oils) and CXS 33-1981 (olive oils), relative density at 20 C/water at 20 C; University of Guelph _Dairy Science and Technology_ eBook (Goff, Hill, Ferrer) for milk and cream densities at 4.4 and 20 C; Eva Crane, _A Book of Honey_ (1980) appendix Table 11 (relative density vs water content, from the IBRA _Comprehensive Survey_), read on a web reproduction; USDA AMS Sucrose Conversion Table (May 2025) with the US (AMS) and Canadian (CFIA) maple-syrup Brix limits; B&G Foods' technical data sheet for Grandma's Original Molasses; Arm & Hammer's sodium bicarbonate specification. All independent of USDA's cup weights.

**Milk Street.** _Flour Power_ (2017): an average of 22 published sources with outliers dropped, "confirmed" by its own measurements, so a meta-average, not independent. _Baking Mix-In Weights_ (2019): 26 ingredients "measured each by volume multiple times", so independent kitchen measurements.

**Rose Levy Beranbaum.** I could not read her books. Her flour figures (sifted / lightly spooned / dip and sweep) were read only as quoted in a 2009 Fresh Loaf forum post, and her cake-flour and cream figures in her Whipped Cream Cake recipe as reprinted on Zoe Bakes; both flagged secondhand.

**Others.** Zojirushi's blog (one brand test: fluffed bread flour 118 g, packed 182 g); Allrecipes (restates 4.25 oz); the American Egg Board egg chart (reproduced on What's Cooking America); USDA AMS 56 egg weight classes; Red Star Yeast's product FAQ; K-State Research and Extension MF3518 and the _Betty Crocker Picture Cooky Book_ (1948) for methods.

**Nutrition labels, and how far to trust them.** 21 CFR 101.9(b)(2)(iii): for bulk products (flour, sugar...) the serving is "the amount in household measure that most closely approximates the reference amount" (RACC, 21 CFR 101.12(b)); (b)(5)(viii): "For nutrition labeling purposes, a teaspoon means 5 milliliters (mL), a tablespoon means 15 mL, a cup means 240 mL"; (b)(7)(ii): grams "rounded to the nearest whole number", to 0.5 g between 2 and 5 g, and to 0.1 g below 2 g. FDA's 1993 _Guidelines for Determining Metric Equivalents of Household Measures_ asks for lab glassware, replicate measurements at 20 C (4 C if refrigerated), and fine solids "leveled using a knife or other straight-edge"; it does not say whether to spoon or dip. So the RACC fixes the _size_ of the serving, not its gram weight, but in practice the declared grams often equal the RACC exactly (King Arthur flours "1/4 cup (30g)" = the 30 g flour RACC; Daisy "2 tbsp (30g)" = the sour-cream RACC; Clabber Girl "1/8 tsp (0.6g)" = the baking-powder RACC), they refer to a 240-mL cup (1.4% larger than the US cup), and a 1-g step on a 12-14 g tablespoon is 7-8%. Labels are weak evidence; I converted them at 236.588/240 and weighted them low. One that departs from its RACC (King Arthur Golden Wheat, 28 g per 1/4 cup) looks genuinely measured.

**Could not use.** Serious Eats (Stella Parks's measuring article would not load: the syndicated copy returned 404 and the seriouseats.com URL I tried was 404); _Joy of Cooking_, _BakeWise_, _The Cake Bible_ (no readable copy); brand pages for Domino, C&H, Quaker, Nestle, Jif, Skippy, Argo, Diamond Crystal and FAGE (blocked or 404), and retailer label pages after the first one. No figures from these are recorded.

**Method US recipes mean.** Most US publishers mean spoon-and-level: King Arthur ('Spoon dry ingredients gently into the cup. Sweep off any excess.'), K-State Extension ('Lightly spoon flour into a graduated measuring cup ... never pack flour down'), Allrecipes (spooning method). America's Test Kitchen / Cook's Illustrated and Milk Street mean dip-and-sweep. Recipes from before the 1960s meant sifted flour (Betty Crocker Picture Cooky Book, 1948: 'Sift flour ... before measuring. Then spoon sifted flour lightly into a "dry" measuring cup').

**Best estimate: 125 g per US cup (spooned and leveled, unsifted).** Why: Spooned sources: HERR 41 126 g from 510 lab measurements (the largest data set anywhere), King Arthur 120 g and Beranbaum 121 g (both aerate the flour first), the KA label 118 g per US cup (RACC-anchored). I weight HERR 41 most and land at 125 g, within 5 g of every spooned source. Dip-and-sweep is a different, well-agreed number: 142 g (ATK 142, HERR 143, Beranbaum 145): offer it when the recipe is from ATK / Cook's Illustrated / Milk Street. Sifted: 116 g (HERR 116, Beranbaum 114). Milk Street's 130 g is an average across methods; FAO entries are industrial, of unknown method, or back-calculated from USDA, so discounted.

## What surprised me

1. **Kitchen-measured liquids come in light.** Vegetable oil: HERR 41 209 g, ATK 210 g, Milk Street 206 g, against 216.6-217.7 g from the Codex densities; no vegetable oil is light enough (they would need 0.87-0.89 g/mL). HERR 41 is also light for honey (325 vs 336 g), molasses (309 vs 332 g), corn syrup (325 g vs 332 g from USDA's own measured specific gravity of light corn syrup in the FAO file), milk (241 vs 243.5 g) and cream (232 vs 235-238.5 g), yet right for a thin liquid like vinegar (239 g). Measured cups of oily or viscous liquids hold short; physics should set liquid conversions.
2. **The 8-oz convention.** King Arthur writes water, milk, cream, sour cream and yogurt as 8 oz = 227 g per cup; ATK writes milk "3/4 cup (6 ounces)" and water "1/3 cup (75 grams)". That is 4% light for water and 7% light for milk.
3. **Packing is the biggest variable among dry goods.** HERR 41's packed brown sugar has an SD of 22.5 g (11%), shredded cheddar 11.4 g (12%); flour's SD is 2-5 g once the method is fixed, but the methods differ by 23% (sifted 116, spooned 126, dipped 143 g for all-purpose).
4. **Dip-and-sweep is not one number either.** For bread flour ATK (156 g) and Beranbaum (157 g) are 15% above HERR 41's dipped 136 g; for whole wheat, ATK's 156 g is 11% above Milk Street's dip-and-sweep 140 g.
5. **The FAO density database is mostly unusable for cups.** Industrial bulk densities (ASI), a crystal density for baking soda (2.2 g/mL, 520 g per cup), USDA back-calculations relabelled as sources (S&W, FNDDS), and no honey or molasses. Its author's own kitchen measurements of flour (0.67 g/mL) and sugar (0.90 g/mL) are far denser than US spooned cups.
6. **Labels mostly echo the RACC** and are defined on a 240-mL cup; see the label note above.
7. **Yeast packet volume:** Red Star and King Arthur say 2 1/4 tsp; the USDA Food Buying Guide says "about 1 tablespoon".
8. **The Egg Board chart contradicts itself:** "3 whole eggs = 1/2 cup", "1 whole egg = 3 tablespoons" and "Large ... 5" eggs to make 1 cup give 6, 5.3 and 5 eggs per cup.
9. **Cream is denser cold:** 1.008 kg/L at 4.4 C against 0.994 at 20 C (Guelph), a 1.4% swing from fat crystallizing.
10. **Oats moved:** King Arthur's 89 g and ATK's 85 g are 13-19% above HERR 41's 75 g (1960s); I can't tell whether flakes or methods changed.

**Where a USDA SR Legacy cup weight might mislead a US baker.** I did not read SR Legacy itself. But HERR 41 says its CFEI measurements fed USDA's household-measure weights, so wherever an SR cup weight matches HERR 41 it carries HERR's methods: (a) all-purpose flour spooned and unsifted, about 126 g, which is 11% light for an ATK, Cook's Illustrated or Milk Street recipe (142 g dip-and-sweep) and 9% heavy for a pre-1960s "sifted flour" recipe (116 g); (b) shredded cheddar lightly spooned, 98 g, 14% under the 4-oz cup recipes and packaging assume (113 g); (c) oil, honey, molasses, milk and cream 1-7% under their densities; (d) 1960s baking-powder formulations (SAS-phosphate 177 g; phosphate 217 g); (e) rolled oats 12-16% under modern test-kitchen figures (ATK 85 g, KA 89 g).
